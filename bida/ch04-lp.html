1. Understand the data first

The Top50Movies dataset contains 50 historical top-ranked movies. There are 36 known budgets and 14 missing budgets, recorded as “--”.

What does a row represent?

One movie is one observation. A column is a variable, such as genre, release year, budget or receipts.

Genre and film classification are categorical. Budget and receipts are quantitative. Ranking records an ordered position; it does not measure a receipt difference.

Check the question and the units

All money is in millions of dollars. Budget and worldwide receipts are nominal. Only the adjusted U.S. receipts column accounts for inflation.

The records describe different movies released in different years. This is not a repeated annual sales series.

Keep the denominator clear. Budget summaries use 36 movies; genre summaries can use all 50. Missing budgets are not zero.
Population, sample and interpretation limits
The records cover the supplied list completely, but form a selected subset of any wider movie market. Top-ranked movies are not a random sample of all films. The sample SD functions used here describe the selected data; they do not remove selection bias. Historical nominal amounts also limit comparisons across years.

2. Choose a statistic for the question

Your questionUseful summaryWhat it tells you
What is the average amount per movie?MeanTotal divided by count. Sensitive to extreme values.
What is a typical value when data are skewed?MedianThe middle ordered value; compare it with the mean and shape.
What is most common?ModeThe most frequent category or numeric value. Ties are possible.
How widely spread are the values?SD, range, IQRSD uses deviations from the mean; range uses endpoints; IQR uses the middle half.

Illustrative budgets: 10, 20, 20, 30 and a final value you choose ($m).

The median stays at $20m in this example. The mean moves as the highest value changes. For the actual 36 known budgets, mean = $54.3m and median = $16.5m.
Variance, SD, CV and geometric mean

Sample variance adds squared deviations from the mean and divides by n − 1. Standard deviation is its square root, so SD returns to the original units. SD is not the exact average absolute distance.

CV needs a meaningful zero and a positive mean away from zero. It is not suitable for Celsius temperatures or a mean near zero.

Illustrative growth: +50% followed by −50% changes $100 to $75. The arithmetic average return is 0%, but the compound annual return is =GEOMEAN(1.5,0.5)-1, about −13.4%. Use positive growth factors, not signed returns.

3. Describe the distribution

Frequency counts observations in a category or interval. Relative frequency divides by the valid count. Percentage expresses that fraction out of 100. Cumulative frequency counts values at or below a threshold.

Read the whole pattern

Check the common range, shape, gaps and tails. A long right tail indicates positive skewness.

Keep empty intervals and use non-overlapping bins. Here a bin includes its lower bound and excludes its upper bound.

Frequency polygon and cumulative percentage
A frequency polygon connects frequencies at the bin midpoints and can help compare distributions. Use relative frequencies when group sizes differ. In the actual budget data, 28 of 36 known budgets (77.8%) are at most $75m.

4. Percentiles, quartiles and IQR

A percentile is a cutoff describing position in ordered data. It is not a percentage of the largest value. Quartiles are the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles.

Q1 · 25th percentile$6.0m
Q2 · median$16.5m
Q3 · 75th percentile$75.0m
IQR · Q3 − Q1$69.0m

IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 75 − 6 = $69m

The central half spans roughly $6m to $75m. IQR describes that central spread and is less sensitive to extremes than the full range.

How these cutoffs are calculated
These examples use Excel’s EXC method: position = k(n + 1), with interpolation between ordered values. For Q3, k = 0.75 and n = 36, so the position is 27.75. Other conventions can give different cutoffs. Ties mean the actual observations do not necessarily split into four equal groups.

5. Read a vertical boxplot

Values increase up the Y-axis. Use the controls to build the plot one component at a time.

What each part means

Box: Q1 to Q3. Its height is the IQR.

Line inside: the median.

Whiskers: the smallest and largest observed values within the fences.

Separate points: observations beyond the fences, flagged for investigation.

The upper fence is $178.5m, but the upper whisker is $150m. A fence is a calculated cutoff. A whisker ends at an actual observation.

6. Detect, check and explain potential outliers

Lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR

Upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

For the actual known budgets:
lower = 6 − 1.5(69) = −$97.5m
upper = 75 + 1.5(69) = $178.5m

Flag values below the lower fence or above the upper fence. A value exactly on a fence is not flagged.

A negative lower fence is a cutoff, not a possible negative movie budget.

Try a fence check

This hypothetical value does not change the movie dataset.

A flag starts an investigation. Check units and recording errors, whether the record belongs in the group, and whether it is a valid exceptional project. Correct verified errors. Keep valid observations and document any exclusion.
Why the comparison group and method matter

A value can be unusual in a compact group but ordinary in a widely spread group. An outlier flag is relative to the selected distribution.

Excel’s Box & Whisker chart offers Inclusive/Exclusive median settings. These are not generally the same as QUARTILE.INC/EXC worksheet functions. Compare the quartile method if a chart and manual flags differ.

7. Position and relationships

z-score

z = (value − mean) ÷ SD

A z-score of +2 means two standard deviations above the mean. It is a location measure, not automatically a probability.

For approximately bell-shaped data, about 68%, 95% and 99.7% lie within 1, 2 and 3 SD of the mean. Do not apply these percentages automatically to skewed budgets.

Scatterplot and correlation

Inspect the scatterplot before calculating a coefficient. Look for direction, form and unusual observations.

For the 36 paired known budgets and worldwide receipts, this indicates a positive linear association within the selected dataset. It does not establish causation.

Covariance and interpretation limits
Covariance’s sign shows the direction of linear association, but its magnitude depends on the units. Correlation standardises the measure to between −1 and +1. A near-zero correlation can hide a strong nonlinear pattern. Historical nominal prices, missing budgets and the selected top-50 list limit generalisation. Receipts are not profit.

Other useful functions

AVERAGE and MEDIAN describe the centre. Use numeric MODE.SNGL or MODE.MULT for modes, and a count table for categories.

MAX(range)-MIN(range), VAR.S(range) and STDEV.S(range) summarise spread. Use .P variants for a complete defined population. A colon selects the full range.

CORREL(budget_range,world_range) and COVARIANCE.S(budget_range,world_range) need the same paired numeric rows.

On a PivotTable, add Title twice to Values: Count for both, then Show Values As → % of Grand Total for the second.
