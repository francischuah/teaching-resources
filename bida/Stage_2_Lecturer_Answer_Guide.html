Lecturer-Only Answer & Teaching Guide

Stage 2: Group Data Analytics Project

Model analysis, Excel setup, PivotTable field arrangements, example outputs, interpretations, managerial implications, and common mistakes.

How to use this guide

Each group generates a different random dataset, so there is no single correct numerical answer. The correct answer is the correct analytical process, correct Excel setup, accurate reading of the group's own output, and a defensible interpretation.

Illustrative numbers only: All tables and values in this guide are examples. Students should not be expected to reproduce these exact figures.

Part A — Data preparation and cleaning

Q1. Inspect blank values in Daily_Promotion_Spend and Total_Sales.

Expected findingModel answer
Blank values occur mainly where Status = Absent.The blanks are expected because absent managers did not perform daily promotional activity and did not record sales for that manager-day.

Q2. Are these blanks errors or logically missing values?

Model answer: They are structurally missing values, not data-entry errors. The missingness is explained by the attendance status.

Q3. Why should blanks not automatically be replaced with zero?

BlankZero
No activity was recorded because the manager was absent.The manager was present, but promotion spend or sales was genuinely zero.
Common mistake: Replacing all blanks with 0 changes the meaning of the data and may lower averages artificially.

Q5. Do sales and spend move together?

Model interpretation: If both monthly spend and monthly sales rise or fall together, this is consistent with a positive association. It does not by itself prove causation.

Common mistake: Writing “promotion spend causes sales” based only on a monthly trend.

Q5. Display and interpret R².

Trendline Options → tick Display R-squared value on chart.

Illustrative R²Interpretation
0.10Spend explains about 10% of the variation in sales; weak explanatory power.
0.35Spend explains about 35% of sales variation; moderate explanatory power.
0.70Spend explains about 70% of sales variation; strong explanatory power.
R² is not a percentage of “sales caused by advertising.” It is the proportion of observed variation in Y accounted for by the fitted linear relationship with X.

Part G — Managerial interpretation

QuestionModel answer framework
Best-performing manager?Use at least two indicators: total sales, average sales per active day, attendance, and/or efficiency.
Manager requiring attention?Support with evidence such as high absenteeism, weak average sales, poor efficiency, or inconsistent relationship.
Is absenteeism serious?Use overall rate, manager differences, and monthly trend.
Best region?Compare total and average sales; note that random region assignment limits causal interpretation.
Does spend relate to sales?Use scatter plot, direction, trendline, and R².
One recommendation?Recommendation must directly follow from evidence, e.g. review high-absence manager, reallocate spend, replicate efficient practices.
Strong final answer example: “Manager 2 appears operationally efficient because the manager records the highest average daily sales and the highest sales generated per RM1 of promotion spend, despite not having the highest total sales. Management may study this manager’s sales practices and consider whether they can be replicated across the team.”

Common mistakes across the entire activity

  • Deleting absent rows before completing attendance analysis.
  • Replacing absent-related blanks with zeros.
  • Using all rows in the sales scatter plot, including blanks.
  • Using Count instead of Sum for Total Sales.
  • Comparing managers only by total sales without considering active days.
  • Using a pie chart for a time trend.
  • Reading R² as proof of causation.
  • Making conclusions that are not supported by the group’s own PivotTables or charts.
