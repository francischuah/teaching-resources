8.1 FUNDAMENTALS OF PROBABILITY

Start with records you can count

Scenario: the café promotion

A café reviews 10 customer records from a promotion. One row describes one customer, counted once. Staff recorded whether that customer bought coffee, used a voucher and was a member.

Question: what share of these recorded customers bought coffee?

A recorded proportion

“6 of these 10 customers bought coffee.”

6 ÷ 10 = 60%

The percentage describes this fixed set of records. The purchases have already happened.

A future probability estimate

“Will the next customer buy coffee?”

Past records may help estimate that chance, but the next outcome is not known. We need relevant data and assumptions about future conditions.

Keep the meaning clear. “60% of these customers bought coffee” is a description. “The next customer has a 60% chance of buying” adds a modelling assumption.
What if we randomly select a record from the table?
If each of the 10 records is equally likely to be selected, the chance of selecting a coffee buyer is 6 out of 10. The records are fixed; uncertainty comes from which one we select. This is different from predicting a new customer.

Three useful counts

Coffee buyers: 6.

Voucher users: 5.

Members: 6.

Customer ID is an identifier, not an amount to average.

AND, OR and NOT

AND finds the overlap. OR includes either group or both. NOT selects the remaining records.

Why do we subtract overlap for OR?
Coffee has 6 customers and voucher has 5. Adding gives 11, because the 3 shared customers were counted twice. Count each person once: 6 + 5 − 3 = 8.

Mutually exclusive: no overlap in the recorded categories

If a transaction records exactly one payment method, Cash and Card cannot both be its category. They are mutually exclusive under that recording rule. Coffee and Voucher are not mutually exclusive because 3 customers belong to both. Real split payments would need a different recording rule.

8.2 CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

“Given” selects the reference group

The important question is “percentage of which total?” A selected group changes the denominator.

Scenario: do members buy coffee?

The café manager focuses on its 6 members. Staff count how many of those 6 bought coffee. The other 4 customers are outside this reference group.

Question: among the members, what proportion bought coffee?

Percentage settingDenominatorRead it as
% of Grand TotalAll 10 customersOf all recorded customers…
% of Row TotalThe selected row’s group sizeAmong members / non-members…
% of Column TotalThe selected column’s group sizeAmong coffee buyers / non-buyers…

Inspect the chosen group

Greyed-out records are outside the selected group. Green records are the coffee buyers within it.

A suitable sentence: “Among the 6 recorded members, 4 bought coffee, so the proportion is 66.7%.” This describes the records, not a guaranteed future conversion rate.
Does a condition always change the percentage?
No. Among the 5 voucher users, 3 bought coffee: 60%. That happens to match the 60% in the full table. Still identify the group and denominator correctly.
9.1 RANDOM & DISCRETE VARIABLES

Does the number label, count or measure?

RoleExampleType
Label1 = cash; 2 = cardCategorical
Count3 enquiries in one hourDiscrete quantitative
Measure3.5 minutes of serviceContinuous quantitative

A count carries quantitative meaning. It should not be grouped with numeric category codes. A measured duration remains a measurement even when recorded as “3 minutes”.

Decide what the number does: label, count or measure.
A small refinement: decimals do not decide the type
Counts are familiar discrete quantities, but other discrete numerical variables can take separated fractional values. A 1–5 satisfaction choice has a fixed set of ordered response categories. Measurements can also be rounded to whole numbers. Interpret what the values represent.

What is a random variable?

It is a numerical outcome not yet known before the process happens. Examples: the number of next semester’s registrations, or the duration of the next service interaction. After the outcome occurs, we can record its observed value.

A distribution describes how values are spread

Illustrative enquiry counts from 12 recorded hours: 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4.

Scenario: enquiries over 12 recorded hours

A university office counts enquiries during each of 12 one-hour intervals. One observation is one hour’s enquiry count. A recorded value of 3 means three enquiries arrived in that hour.

Question: which counts were common across those recorded hours?

An observed frequency distribution describes these past records. A probability distribution can instead model possible future outcomes.

Recognise two useful count models

Scenario: registrations after 10 offers

The admissions office sends 10 offers. Each applicant either registers or does not register. Before the replies arrive, the office wants to describe possible total registrations, from 0 to 10.

Assumptions for this illustration: the same 45% registration chance for each applicant and independent decisions.

Scenario: enquiries during the next hour

The service office needs to plan staff for the next one-hour period. The number of incoming enquiries is unknown and is not limited to a fixed set of applicants.

Assumptions for this illustration: 3 enquiries per hour on average, a constant rate and independent arrivals.

Binomial: count successes in a fixed number of trials. Poisson: count occurrences during a stated interval. The model assumptions matter; no formula memorisation is needed here.
Other discrete distributions
A custom distribution can use relevant historical frequencies. In a discrete uniform model, a finite set of outcomes has equal probabilities, such as a fair draw among numbered tickets. The ticket numbers label the outcomes; do not interpret a larger ticket number as a larger business outcome.

Now measure duration

Scenario: time one service interaction

Staff start timing when assistance begins and stop when the interaction ends. One observation is one completed interaction. A duration of 6.5 means 6½ minutes.

Question: which duration ranges are most common among these 12 illustrative measurements?

Illustrative service measurements include 5.5, 6.5, 7.5 and 8.5 minutes.

A histogram groups measurements into intervals. In this chart, include each interval’s lower limit and exclude its upper limit.

Being continuous does not automatically make a variable normal. Inspect the pattern and context first.

9.2 NORMAL DISTRIBUTIONS

Recognise the bell-shaped pattern

Scenario: propose a model for service duration

The office wants a simple model for a future service duration. For this illustration, suppose a symmetric bell-shaped approximation is reasonable, with a mean of 7.5 minutes and SD of 1.5 minutes.

These are demonstration assumptions, not estimates fitted from the 12 illustrative durations.

A normal model is symmetric around its centre, with more concentration near the mean and less far away.

Use it when the measurement pattern and business context support a bell-shaped approximation.

This illustration assumes a mean service duration of 7.5 minutes and SD of 1.5 minutes. These are demonstration parameters, not estimates from a supplied dataset.

Explore centre and spread

Scenario: compare service consistency

Two proposed service processes can have the same average duration but different spreads. Keep the mean at 7.5 minutes first, then change SD.

Question: which pattern has durations more consistently close to the mean? Afterwards, move the mean to explore a change in typical duration.

The mean tells us about the centre. Standard deviation tells us about spread. Lower spread means greater consistency around that mean, but does not by itself prove that a process is stable over time.

Optional: interpret a modelled proportion

For the fixed illustrative model with mean 7.5 and SD 1.5 minutes, choose a threshold.

Scenario: a 10-minute service target

The manager asks what proportion of future interactions might exceed a 10-minute target. Use the stated normal model, with mean 7.5 and SD 1.5 minutes.

Read the graph: choose the target on the horizontal axis. The shaded area to its right is the modelled proportion beyond that target.

Excel link and curve interpretation

For a threshold of 10 minutes, =1-NORM.DIST(10,7.5,1.5,TRUE) gives about 4.8%.

Probability is the area over an interval, not the curve’s height. This is a model estimate. A percentage counted from observed cases is a different calculation.

Other continuous patterns: recognition only

The syllabus emphasises normal distributions. These short comparisons help you recognise that not every measurement has a bell shape.
