Research Design

A framework proposes relationships. Research design explains how we will investigate them. This chapter connects the research question to choices about evidence, manipulation, setting, strategy, unit of analysis and time.

1

The blueprint for investigating a question

Research design is a blueprint for collecting, measuring and analysing data, based on the research questions.

Chapters 3–4 defined the problem and examined knowledge. Chapter 5 proposed relationships. Now ask: what evidence will help answer the question, and how can we obtain it?

Continuing question

Is supervisor support related to turnover intention?

Important distinction

Actual turnover means leaving. Turnover intention means thinking about or planning to leave.

Consider access, time, resources and ethical obligations. A more complex design is not automatically the most appropriate one.

Seven decisions: purpose, investigation, researcher interference, setting, strategy, unit of analysis and time horizon.

2

Purpose: what do we want to learn?

Exploratory

Understand an unclear issue. Example: interview employees to explore why they consider leaving.

Descriptive

Describe conditions or characteristics. Example: describe current turnover-intention levels across departments.

Hypothesis testing

Examine a proposed relationship. Example: test the association between support and turnover intention.

A project may have several phases. Explain the purpose of the phase you are designing. Describing a difference does not establish why it exists.
3

Correlational or causal investigation?

Correlational

Are variables associated? Measure existing supervisor support and turnover intention.

Causal

Does changing something produce a change in an outcome? Investigate whether introducing a support programme reduces intention.

Comparing a programme with usual arrangements

Programme group

Measure before → introduce the programme → measure after.

Comparison group

Measure before → usual arrangements → measure after.

Address competing explanations. Random allocation, where feasible, strengthens group comparability.

Association alone does not establish causation. A before-and-after change in one group can also have other explanations.
Think: does a survey prove that support reduces intention?

No. A survey can identify an association. The design must address alternative explanations before supporting a causal claim.

4

How much will the researcher interfere?

Minimal

Observe or measure existing conditions without manipulating the IV.

Moderate

Introduce a change in a natural setting.

Excessive / high

Use extensive manipulation and control in an artificial setting.

“Excessive” is the course terminology for high experimental control, not a judgement that the research is unethical.

5

Where will the study take place?

Field studyNatural setting + no manipulation
Field experimentNatural setting + manipulation
Lab experimentArtificial setting + manipulation

A workplace survey of existing support is a field study. Introducing a support programme at work can form a field experiment. Assigning controlled scenarios during a simulated task can form a lab experiment.

Two distinct questions: Is the environment natural or artificial? Does the researcher manipulate conditions?
Try it: a researcher introduces an incentive at a working restaurant. What setting fits?

Field experiment, assuming the incentive is a researcher-introduced manipulation and the restaurant continues operating in its normal environment.

6

Choose a strategy that fits the question

Experiment

Manipulate a condition and compare outcomes. Useful for causal questions.

Survey

Collect standardised information from or about people to describe or examine relationships.

Observation

Systematically watch and record behaviour or events.

Case study

Investigate a bounded case, such as an organisation or event, in depth.

Grounded theory

Develop an explanation inductively through systematic engagement with data.

Action research

Use cycles of planning, action, observation and reflection to improve practice.

Mixed methods: connect qualitative and quantitative evidence

Interviews could explore employees’ experiences, then inform a survey. Alternatively, interviews could help interpret survey findings. Explain how and why the evidence is integrated.

A survey is not automatically descriptive. A strategy can serve different purposes; its use must fit the question.
7

Unit of analysis: what entity are we analysing?

The unit of analysis is the entity about which the study analyses data and draws conclusions.

Individual: employeeDyad: supervisor–employee pairGroup: work teamOrganisation: restaurantCulture: cultural group

Employee-level question

Employees report support and turnover intention. Analyse differences between employees.

Restaurant-level question

Managers provide restaurant information. Compare restaurant efficiency.

Respondent ≠ necessarily the unit of analysis. A manager can be the informant while the restaurant is the unit being analysed.
8

Time horizon: snapshot or evidence across time?

Cross-sectional

Measure relevant constructs at one point or during one data-collection period.

Longitudinal

Investigate constructs or change across several points in time.

Repeated cross-sectionsDifferent samples at different times
Panel studyThe same units followed over time
Choose timing independently of setting. A field study can be longitudinal. An experiment can take place within a single session.
Think: the same employees complete a survey every three months. Is this an experiment?

Not necessarily. It is a longitudinal panel if the same employees are followed. Without a manipulation, repeated surveys do not become an experiment.

10

Apply the choices to a business scenario

These scenarios adapt the exercises in your course slides. State assumptions and justify your choices.

A · Boutique comparison

Joyce wants to compare her boutique with similar businesses on technology use, sales volume, profit margin and staff training.

Reveal a defensible boutique design

Assuming a comparison of current business characteristics: primarily descriptive; minimal interference; field study; a survey or records comparison; businesses as the unit of analysis; cross-sectional if information is collected once.

An exploratory phase may help identify what to compare. Mixed methods is appropriate only when a qualitative contribution and its integration are justified.

B · Restaurant incentives

Paul wants to know whether an incentive plan improves restaurant efficiency before introducing it widely.

Reveal a possible restaurant design

Assuming a trial with suitable comparison restaurants: hypothesis testing; causal investigation; moderate interference; field experiment; experimental strategy; restaurant-level analysis if comparing restaurant efficiency; longitudinal if measured before and after.

Address other differences between restaurants. Random allocation can help where feasible. Other proposed designs need their own rationale.

If Paul introduces an incentive in operating restaurants, which setting fits?

Answer and explanation

Field experiment. The researcher introduces a manipulation in a natural setting. Simply being inside a restaurant does not make the study a non-experimental field study.
