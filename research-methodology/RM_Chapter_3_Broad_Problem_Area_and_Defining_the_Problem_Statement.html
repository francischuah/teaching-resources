Broad Problem Area & Defining the Problem Statement

Business research often begins because something is happening. Sales may be declining, employee turnover may be increasing, customers may be dissatisfied, or employees may not be using a newly introduced system. But what we observe is not necessarily the actual research problem.

From a Broad Problem Area to a Research Problem

A research problem does not necessarily mean something has gone seriously wrong. Research may begin because:

A difficulty needs attention

An organisation is experiencing a problem.

There is room to improve

An existing situation could be made better.

Something is not understood

We need a clearer explanation of what is happening.

A question remains unanswered

Previous research leaves something to investigate.

CURRENT / ACTUAL STATE
Turnover = 18%
↓   GAP   ↓
DESIRED / PREFERRED STATE
Lower / normal turnover

In our example, normal turnover is approximately 8%. The increase to 18% indicates something may require investigation. We still do not know why turnover has increased.

Identifying that something is happening is only the beginning of the research process.
2

Symptom ≠ Real Problem

OBSERVATION / SYMPTOM
8% → 18%

Employee turnover increased.

POSSIBLE EXPLANATION / ASSUMPTION

“Employees are leaving because salaries are too low.”

Low salary has not yet been established as the cause.

Other possible explanations also deserve attention:

  • Workload
  • Supervisor support
  • Career opportunities
  • Working conditions
  • Rewards and pay
  • Organisational climate

Researchers should investigate before concluding.

Preliminary Information Gathering

Preliminary information gathering is the early collection of information to gain a wider understanding of the situation before defining the research problem.

It helps researchers understand what is happening, decide whether the issue is worth investigating, identify possible explanations, narrow the broad problem area and develop a more precise problem statement.

OBSERVE
→
INVESTIGATE
→
UNDERSTAND
→
NARROW
→
DEFINE

Organisational / Contextual Information

  • Organisational background and structure
  • People involved and available resources
  • Performance information
  • Relevant organisational conditions

Existing Knowledge / Literature

  • What do previous studies already tell us?
  • What explanations have been investigated?
  • What remains unclear or insufficiently understood?

Possible sources

  • Managers
  • Employees
  • Informal interviews
  • Organisational records
  • Reports
  • Previous research
The objective is not yet to conduct the entire study. Understand the situation well enough to define what should actually be investigated.
4

Try It: What Is the Real Problem?

Low digital-system usage

A company introduces a new digital system. Six months later, employee usage remains low. Management concludes: “Our employees resist technology.”

Pause and think: What do we actually know? What is management assuming? What else should we investigate?

Compare your answer

What we know

Employee usage of the system is low.

What management assumes

Employees resist technology.

Questions to investigate

  • Was sufficient training provided?
  • Is the system easy to use?
  • Do employees find it useful?
  • Is management supporting its use?
  • Does the system increase workload?
Do not confuse an observed situation with an explanation for that situation.

Problems Mean Gaps

PROBLEMS MEAN GAPS

A practical gap and a knowledge gap answer different questions.

PRACTICAL GAP

Reality differs from a preferred condition.

What is happening: Turnover = 18%.

What we would prefer: Lower / normal turnover.

KNOWLEDGE GAP

Existing research does not adequately answer or explain something.

What existing research tells us versus what we still need to understand.

PRACTICAL GAP
What is happening in reality?
+
KNOWLEDGE GAP
What do we still not adequately know?
↓
RESEARCH NEED
A strong research problem establishes both why the issue matters and what still needs to be understood.
6

Building the Problem Statement

The problem statement becomes the focal point of the research: a clear, focused account of what needs investigation and why the study is needed.

  1. Practical Problem

    What is happening and why does it matter?

  2. Scientific / Knowledge Problem

    What does research still not adequately explain?

  3. 30,000-foot Review

    Provide a concise overview of what previous studies have investigated and generally established.

  4. Spot the Gap

    What remains unclear, inconsistent, insufficiently explained or insufficiently examined?

  5. Current Study

    What specifically will this study address?

PRACTICAL PROBLEM → KNOWLEDGE PROBLEM → WHAT WE KNOW → WHAT IS MISSING → WHAT THIS STUDY WILL ADDRESS
A good problem statement should lead the reader logically to the need for the research.
7

What Can a Research Gap Look Like?

Research gap ≠ “Nobody has ever studied this.”

Knowledge Gap

Something remains insufficiently understood or explained.

Methodological Gap

Previous methods or designs leave an important issue unresolved.

Contextual Gap

Existing evidence may not adequately explain another relevant population, industry, setting or context.

Findings may be inconsistent, or previous studies may identify limitations requiring further investigation.

Be careful with “No previous study has examined…” This is a very strong claim. More defensible wording may include “Limited research has examined…” or “The relationship remains insufficiently understood…”. These claims still need evidence.
The research gap must be supported by the literature, not simply claimed.
8

What Makes a Good Research Problem?

RELEVANT

Does it matter?

Addresses a meaningful practical or academic need.

FEASIBLE

Can I actually study it?

Fits my skills, access, time and resources.

INTERESTING

Is it worth pursuing?

Sustains my interest and motivation to complete the research.

RELEVANT · FEASIBLE · INTERESTING

From Problem Statement to Research Question

Once the problem is clearly defined, ask: What exactly do we need to learn? This becomes the Research Question (RQ).

A research question translates the research problem into a specific need for information.
PROBLEM
→
WHAT DO WE NEED TO KNOW?
→
RESEARCH QUESTION

Research issue: Employee rewards may be related to employee motivation.

Research Question: Does employee reward influence employee motivation?

10

Research Question ↔ Research Objective

RESEARCH QUESTION — WHAT?

What do we want to learn?

Does employee reward influence employee motivation?

RESEARCH OBJECTIVE — PURPOSE

What will the study examine?

To examine the effect of employee reward on employee motivation.

RQ ↔ RO
SAME RESEARCH INTENTION

The question and objective should match in their concepts, relationship and focus.

RQ: Does employee training influence job performance?
✕ NOT ALIGNED

RO: To examine employee satisfaction with salary.

This objective examines different concepts from the research question.

✓ ALIGNED

Corrected RO: To examine the effect of employee training on job performance.

If the Research Question changes, the Research Objective should change with it.
11

Set the Boundaries: Research Scope

Scope establishes what the study covers — and what it does not cover.

INSIDE THE BOUNDARY

What this study investigates. Specify the relevant boundaries:

Purpose / TopicWhat is being investigated?
Population / SampleWho is included?
Geographical SettingWhere is the study conducted?
Time PeriodWhich period does it cover?
Concepts / TheoriesWhich ideas are included?

OUTSIDE THE BOUNDARY

What this study does not attempt to investigate: other topics, populations, locations, periods or concepts beyond its stated focus.

A clearly defined scope keeps research focused and manageable.
12

Why Does the Study Matter? Significance

The significance of the study explains why conducting the research is worthwhile. Ask: Who may benefit from what we learn?

Academic / Knowledge Value

The study may:

  • Address an identified knowledge gap
  • Extend existing understanding
  • Contribute additional evidence

Practical Value

Findings may help managers, organisations, institutions, industries, policymakers or relevant stakeholders.

Not every study needs to benefit every group.

Significance should follow logically from the research problem. Explain the potential benefit and who it matters to.

Revision: What Must You Know?

Try answering each question before reading the explanation.

  1. What is the difference between a symptom and a research problem?

    A symptom tells us what we observe. The research problem identifies what needs to be investigated.

  2. Why do researchers gather preliminary information?

    To understand and narrow the situation before defining the research problem.

  3. What does “Problems Mean Gaps” mean?

    Research may begin from a practical gap, a knowledge gap, or the connection between them.

  4. How can we build a problem statement?

    Practical Problem → Knowledge Problem → 30,000-foot Review → Gap → Current Study.

  5. What is the relationship between RQ and RO?

    They should express the same research intention.

  6. What is the difference between scope and significance?

    Scope = boundaries of the study.
    Significance = why the study matters.

Key Takeaway

SYMPTOM ≠ PROBLEM

Do not assume that what you first observe tells you the cause.

PROBLEMS MEAN GAPS

Connect the practical problem with what we still need to understand.

ALIGNMENT MATTERS

Problem → RQ ↔ RO → Scope → Significance should tell one coherent research story.
