Five questions for evaluating a study

Evaluate its contribution to your research—not simply whether you like its conclusion.
01 · RelevanceDoes it address my concepts, question or context?
02 · CredibilityWho produced it? Where and how was it published?
03 · EvidenceDo the methods and data support the conclusions?
04 · CurrencyDo I need recent evidence, foundational work, or both?
05 · LimitationsWhat can it explain—and what remains uncertain?

CHAPTER 4 · LITERATURE REVIEW

Citation counts are clues, not verdicts

A frequently cited study may be worth examining—but still needs evaluation.
WHAT THEY CAN SUGGEST

A study has received attention

Follow how other researchers use, extend or challenge it.

WHAT THEY CANNOT ESTABLISH

That the study is correct or relevant

Older work has had more time to accumulate citations. New research may have few.

Also ask: Is the evidence appropriate for the claim I want to make?
A different country or a small sample is not automatically a weakness.

One-time questionnaire survey.

Which would you include, and with what caution?
Use both if relevant. They show agreement is not universal. Check the full methods and context; these summaries do not establish why findings differ. Neither survey proves causation.

Documenting the literature review

The written review has three connected jobs.
01

Introduce the subject

Define the relevant concepts and establish what the review covers.

02

Highlight the problem

Explain why the issue matters and what needs to be understood.

03

Summarise the work so far

Compare relevant findings and show how they inform the proposed study.

Your reader should understand what is known, what remains unclear and why your study is needed.

1 · Turnover intention: meaning and relevance

WITHIN EACH THEME

Claim → Evidence → Comparison → Implication

Use several relevant studies to develop an idea. Explain how the evidence connects to your research.

Select the structure that fits your problem; there is no need to list every article.

CHAPTER 4 · LITERATURE REVIEW

Summary versus synthesis

Fictional practice studies · Study labels are teaching placeholders, not valid citations.
AUTHOR-BY-AUTHOR SUMMARY

Study A found an association between supervisor support and turnover intention. Study B found a similar association. Study C found no clear association.

Reports each finding, but leaves the comparison to the reader.
CONNECTED SYNTHESIS

Evidence from service and retail samples suggests that greater supervisor support is associated with lower turnover intention (Studies A and B). However, Study C found no clear association among hotel employees. The findings differ across these samples, but the summaries alone do not explain why.

Synthesis combines evidence while preserving differences and uncertainty.

Make the gap follow the evidence

A gap needs support from the literature you have actually examined.
WEAK CLAIM

“Nobody has studied this.”

Three articles cannot establish that no previous research exists.

CAREFUL INTERPRETATION

“These practice findings differ.”

Check the wider literature to establish what remains unresolved.

A possible study direction: examine the relationship in a clearly justified setting.
A new setting alone does not automatically justify a study.
Keep the sequence connected: Evidence → Unresolved issue → Current study.

Keep your words and sources traceable

Document both your thinking and where the evidence came from.
READING NOTES

Separate three things

The author’s idea · an exact quotation · your own interpretation.

WRITING

Paraphrase the meaning

Read, understand, write in your own words, then check accuracy and acknowledge the source.

DOCUMENTATION

Connect citation and reference

Use the course’s APA 7 guidance. Check that each cited source has its matching reference entry.

Changing a few words is not sufficient paraphrasing. A paraphrased idea still needs a citation.

Write one connected paragraph

Use the fictional A–B–C matrix. In your assignment, use real sources and accurate citations.
3 minutes

Include three moves

1 · State the shared finding.
2 · Explain the difference and a limitation.
3 · Identify what needs closer investigation.

Studies A and B associate greater supervisor support with lower turnover intention, whereas Study C reports no clear association. These one-time surveys do not demonstrate causation. Further reading is needed to establish whether the differences reflect context, measurement or other explanations.
