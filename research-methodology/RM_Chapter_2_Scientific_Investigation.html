What is scientific investigation?

Chapter 1 gave us the broad business-research logic:

→
Question
→
Evidence
→
Analysis
→
Decision
Scientific investigation is a systematic, logical and rigorous process of identifying a problem, collecting and analysing evidence, and drawing conclusions from that evidence.
Key idea: “Scientific” does not mean “always correct”. It means the investigation is conducted systematically and carefully so that the evidence and conclusions are more credible.
Why is scientific investigation important?

It helps researchers investigate problems systematically, produce findings with greater accuracy and confidence, base conclusions on evidence, identify important factors requiring attention, compare findings with other investigations, and support better-informed decisions.

What makes research scientific?

You do not need to see eight long definitions at once. Start with the idea: good scientific research has a clear purpose, is carefully conducted, can be tested and checked, and draws conclusions from evidence.

PURPOSE

Purposiveness

A clear, well-defined problem or purpose.

CARE

Rigor

Appropriate theory, design, measures and procedures.

TEST

Testability

Predictions can be examined using evidence.

CHECK

Replicability

Others can repeat the study and examine whether similar findings occur.

Show the remaining four hallmarks

Precision & Confidence

Recognize the accuracy and uncertainty associated with estimates and findings.

Objectivity

Conclusions follow the evidence rather than personal preference.

Generalizability

Consider whether findings can apply beyond the immediate study.

Parsimony

Prefer an adequate explanation without unnecessary complexity.

3

Can business research be 100% scientific?

Business researchers often study people and organizations. Feelings, attitudes, perceptions and behaviour are not perfectly controllable or directly measurable.
Example: measuring employee job satisfaction

We cannot directly observe “satisfaction” like we measure height. We may use survey questions, but responses can be influenced by interpretation, experience, mood, honesty and the work environment.

Other obstacles

Researchers may also have difficulty obtaining a truly representative sample, which can restrict how widely the findings can be generalized.

So why bother? Scientific business research does not promise perfect certainty. It gives us a disciplined way to produce more credible evidence.
4

Why are we doing the research?

BASIC RESEARCH

“We want to know more.”

Main purpose: discover, develop or expand knowledge.

Example: What factors influence employees’ willingness to share knowledge at work?

APPLIED RESEARCH

“We need to understand or solve this problem.”

Main purpose: understand or solve a specific organizational problem.

Example: Why has knowledge sharing declined in Company ABC, and what can management do?

Important: Both basic and applied research can be scientific. The main difference is the purpose and context of the research.

Theory ⇄ Reality: how does reasoning work?

A theory provides an explanation of how and why something happens. Research creates a conversation between our explanation and what we actually observe.

THEORY
our explanation / prediction
⇄
REALITY / DATA
what we observe
What are the building blocks of theory?

WHAT

What are the important factors?

HOW

How are those factors related?

WHY

Why does the relationship occur?

WHO / WHERE / WHEN

Under what conditions does the explanation apply?

Deduction · THEORY → DATA

Theory → Hypothesis → Collect & Analyse Data → Decision

Start with an existing explanation and test a specific prediction.

See an example

Theory suggests stronger supervisor support is linked with higher job satisfaction. Develop H1, collect employee data, then test whether the evidence supports the prediction.

Induction · DATA → THEORY

Observe → Patterns → Relationships → Theory

Start with observations and build a broader explanation.

See an example

Employees repeatedly mention poor recognition, workload and lack of support in interviews. The researcher identifies patterns and develops a broader explanation of dissatisfaction.

Memory trick: Deduction tests theory against reality. Induction builds explanation from observations of reality.
6

How can we investigate?

Quantitative

Numbers & variables. Useful for measurement, relationships, comparisons and hypothesis testing.

Example: Does supervisor support influence job satisfaction?

Qualitative

Meaning & experience. Useful for deeper understanding through interviews, observations or text.

Example: How do employees describe supervisor support?

Mixed Methods

Both. Combine quantitative and qualitative evidence when this helps answer the problem.

Example: Survey first, then interview employees to explain the patterns.

Key idea: There is no universally “best” approach. The research problem and information needed help determine the appropriate approach.
7

Research paradigms — keep it simple

For this chapter, think of a paradigm as a broad way of thinking about how knowledge about the world can be obtained.

Positivism

MEASURE

Phenomena can be systematically observed, measured and classified.

Constructivism

UNDERSTAND MEANING

People interpret and construct meaning from their experiences and social world.

Pragmatism

WHAT WORKS?

Use approaches that help answer the problem at hand.

Common associations — but not rigid rules

Positivism is commonly associated with quantitative research; constructivism with qualitative research; and pragmatism with mixed methods. Treat these as useful associations, not one-to-one rules.

Revision: what must you know?

You should be able to explain scientific investigation, the eight hallmarks, obstacles in behavioural research, basic vs applied research, deduction vs induction, the three research approaches, the three paradigms, and the seven-step hypothetico-deductive process.

Q1 · What makes an investigation “scientific”?

It follows a systematic, logical and rigorous process, collects and analyses evidence, and draws conclusions based on that evidence.

Q2 · Basic or applied? A researcher studies motivation mainly to expand knowledge.

Basic research. The main purpose is to develop or expand knowledge.

Q3 · Basic or applied? Company ABC investigates falling motivation to solve an immediate problem.

Applied research. It addresses a specific organizational problem.

Q4 · Theory → hypothesis → survey data → test. Deductive or inductive?

Deductive. The researcher begins with theory and tests a prediction using evidence.

Q5 · Put the seven steps in order.

Broad problem area → Problem statement → Hypotheses → Measures → Data collection → Data analysis → Interpretation.

KEY TAKEAWAY
Scientific investigation is not about producing perfect or unquestionable answers. It is about using a systematic, logical and rigorous process to connect theory with evidence so that our conclusions are more credible.
